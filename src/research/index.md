# Research

<p class="lead">How and why do people experience emotions? What are the consequences of emotions and of the ways we manage them? The subjective experience of emotions (i.e., feelings) is incredibly interesting and very human, along with the propensities and abilities to regulate emotions. My training in mindfulness, impulsivity, and emotion regulation led to an interdisciplinary approach to affective science. My questions increasingly bridge between affective science and environmental psychology. I have expertise in psychophysiology and experimental methods, and a commitment to transparent and reproducible practices.</p>

## Emotion regulation and climate change

People feel many emotions about climate change, but those emotions may or may not help us solve the problem. It may be because climate change is a social dilemma, a problem of balancing group and individual interests. However, it may also be important to consider that climate change can't be perceived through the senses like many other threats. As we experience more disasters and suffering related to climate change, climate emotions might even turn from a motivating force into an inhibitory one.  

For my dissertation at Tufts University, I developed a conceptual framework describing how emotion and emotion regulation are associated with climate change belief and climate change solutions. To study these questions, I created climate change information stimuli by rephrasing statements of fact from an Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) report, keeping the report's scientific confidence levels, and pairing each statement with a related image. I have used these materials to record peripheral physiological responses in the lab, in an online study with people who do and do not believe in climate change, and to test emotion regulation in a simulated social dilemma. The simulation involved decisions to donate that would mitigate the risk of climate disaster.

## Psychophysiology

I use electroencephalography (EEG) and peripheral physiological measures to study emotion and emotion regulation in the brain and body. I am particularly interested in event-related potentials (ERPs) as measures of attention, appraisal, motivation, and emotional arousal. Much of my work focuses on the late positive potential (LPP), which is a complex signal of relevance, emotion, and motivation. 

At Swarthmore I work with two EEG systems (EGI and Brain Products) and have trained more than two dozen students on standard operating procedures I wrote. My current research practicum is running an EEG study that uses reappraisal and suppression to try to separate emotional and motivational signals in the LPP.

## Earlier and collaborative work

My early research focused on mindfulness and its social and emotional effects, including ERP studies of disgust regulation and studies of prosocial behavior after contemplative practice. Between degrees, I worked as staff on clinical behavioral health projects at the Richmond VA Medical Center and Virginia Commonwealth University, on topics from community violence prevention to impulsivity, substance use, and traumatic injury.

In collaborative work at Tufts, I studied how time pressure affects stress and performance, including [route planning on maps](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0316382) and [fine motor tasks](https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0001386). At Swarthmore I've recently started collaborations with faculty and students on attentional biases in pain perception and on peripheral physiological signals of positive and negative awe.
